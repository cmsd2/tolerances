# 00 — Orientation

## The problem

You have an assembly. Some parts are FDM-printed, some are PCBs from a fab house, some are
bought (screws, bearings, connectors, magnets). Every one of them differs from its nominal
dimension. You want to know, *before* committing to a print run or a PCB order, whether the
parts will go together and function.

That question decomposes into three:

1. **Does it fit?** Is there always a non-negative clearance where one is needed, and never
   more clearance than the function tolerates?
2. **How often does it not fit?** A 1-in-3 failure rate is a redesign; a 1-in-500 failure
   rate on a part you print one of is irrelevant.
3. **Which dimension is to blame?** If it fails, you need to know which two or three of the
   fifteen contributing dimensions actually drive the outcome, because those are the only
   ones worth tightening.

Everything in this document set exists to answer those three questions with numbers you can
defend.

## What commercial tools actually sell

RD8's headline feature is "Interface X-ray": it reads a STEP file, finds mating features,
and *constructs the tolerance chain for you*. CETOL 6σ and 3DCS do the same thing more
elaborately, with variational solid-model kernels. That automation is real and it is the
expensive part.

What they compute once the chain exists is:

- worst-case: a sum of absolute values
- RSS: a square root of a sum of squares
- Monte Carlo: sample, evaluate, histogram

These are, respectively, one line, one line, and roughly fifteen lines of NumPy. Their value
is not in the arithmetic.

So the honest question is not "how do I replicate RD8 for free" but **"which parts of RD8's
value do I actually need at my scale?"** For a multipart FreeCAD assembly with, say, three
to eight parts and a handful of critical fits, the answer is: not the automation. You have
few enough chains that you can identify them by hand, and identifying them by hand is a
better use of your attention than trusting a tool to find them.

## The three things that actually matter

### 1. Capability data

A tolerance analysis is a function of its inputs. If you type ±0.2 mm into a Monte Carlo
engine because a blog said FDM holds ±0.2 mm, you have produced a beautifully rendered
histogram of a guess. Your printer has a specific, measurable, *asymmetric* and *directional*
error signature: external dimensions typically run oversize, holes typically run undersize,
Z behaves differently from XY, and error scales with feature size.

No software can give you this. You get it by printing coupons and measuring them. It is the
single highest-value thing in this document set, and it is covered in
[04 — Process capability](04-process-capability.md).

### 2. A single source of truth

The characteristic failure mode of hobby (and a great deal of professional) mechanical work
is not bad maths. It is that the boss height is 5.0 mm in the FreeCAD sketch, 5.2 mm in the
spreadsheet you did the stack-up in, and 5.0 mm in the note you wrote three weeks ago about
why it needed to be 5.4 mm. Then the PCB outline changes and nothing downstream notices.

The fix is boring and it works: one version-controlled file that holds every dimension,
every tolerance, and the provenance of each. FreeCAD reads from it. KiCad reads from it. The
analysis reads from it. Nothing has a private copy. See
[05 — Parameter model](05-parameter-model.md).

### 3. A chain you can defend

A tolerance stack-up is an argument. "This gap is at least zero because these six dimensions
combine in this way, each contributing this much, under these assumptions about
independence." When it turns out not to be at least zero, you go back to the argument and
find which premise was wrong. If the chain was auto-generated by a tool and you never
inspected it, you have nothing to go back to.

Writing the chain by hand, in a file, with a comment explaining each term, is a feature.

## Two things specific to 3D printing that generic tools get wrong

These are worth flagging up front because they invalidate the default settings of every
tolerance package on the market.

**Error is systematic, not random.** Classical statistical tolerancing assumes each dimension
is a normally distributed random variable centred on nominal. FDM is not like that. A given
printer, material and slicer profile produces a repeatable *bias* — say, holes 0.15 mm
undersize — plus a comparatively small random spread. Modelling that as ±0.2 mm symmetric
about nominal is wrong in both directions: it overstates the randomness and hides the
offset. Model it as (offset, spread). See [04](04-process-capability.md) and
[02 §6](02-methods.md).

**Features on the same part are correlated.** RSS and naive Monte Carlo assume independence.
But if three of the dimensions in your chain are all Z-heights on parts printed on the same
machine in the same session, they share a common first-layer-squish and Z-step bias. They
will move *together*, which means the variation stacks arithmetically, not in quadrature.
Scholz names this exact hazard: *"several similar/same parts (coming from the same process)
could be used in the same assembly. If this process is affected by a mean shift, then this
mean shift will accumulate in worst case fashion."* This is the most common way a
statistical tolerance analysis of a 3D-printed assembly comes out optimistic.
[05](05-parameter-model.md) puts correlation groups in the schema for this reason.

## What to skip

- **Don't buy tolerance-analysis software.** At your scale the licence cost buys nothing the
  free path doesn't give you.
- **Don't buy the ISO standards yet.** They are ~CHF 100–200 each and several are only
  meaningful as a set. [03](03-standards.md) lists what is free and what is worth money.
- **Don't do GD&T theatre.** Applying position/profile callouts to a printed part you will
  never inspect on a CMM adds ceremony, not information. Learn enough GD&T to think clearly
  about datums and to read a supplier's drawing; apply it to your own drawings only where a
  supplier will actually act on it.
- **Don't Monte Carlo before you have measured anything.** A simulation over invented inputs
  is a way of laundering a guess into a number with three decimal places. Coupons first.

## Scope of these documents

**In scope:** FDM/FFF printing, FreeCAD parametric assemblies, KiCad PCBs and their
mechanical interfaces, bought fasteners and components, 1D and simple 2D tolerance chains,
statistical and worst-case methods, process capability measurement with calipers and a
micrometer.

**Out of scope:** resin/SLA and SLS specifics (much of [04](04-process-capability.md) still
applies, the error signatures differ), full 3D variational analysis with rotational degrees
of freedom (pointers in [02 §8](02-methods.md)), compliant/deformable part analysis, thermal
and time-dependent variation beyond a first-order treatment, formal CMM inspection.
